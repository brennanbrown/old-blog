The world only exists in your eyes, until you write it into being. Each morning I wake before the sun to perform this sacred act of attention — brewing coffee in a Chemex while watching frost flowers bloom across my bedroom windows. The glass separating me from a Calgary winter that philosopher Maurice Merleau-Ponty might say *isn’t what I think, but what I live through*. What I survive through. The particular squeak of boots on snow at -15°C, the way traffic lights refract through ice crystals into miniature prismatic galaxies, the smell of diesel exhaust crystallized in cold air like frozen sweetgrass smoke. Winter here bites. Devours. Transforms.

Let me be clear: this is a story about wanting to be seen. About making meaning in a world overflowing with content. About choosing to create anyway.

#### The Price of Community

My laptop glows in Element Café on 17th Avenue, screen reflecting exposed brick walls and Edison bulbs signifying a carefully curated authenticity. Two tables over, a woman rehearses a PowerPoint presentation about quarterly earnings, her voice carrying the same cadence as my professors once used discussing Derrida — the difference and deferral of meaning, like my own endlessly deferred dreams of literary recognition. The Wi-Fi password is “community”. All lowercase, no spaces. Everything in this city feels lowercase lately, except the rent. The coffee costs too much. I pay it anyway. Some rituals are worth the price.

The streets between cafés and bookstores form their own story map. Each corner holds history. Each intersection marks a memory. I trace these paths daily, as if repetition might somehow make them mine.

I’ve been thinking about Walter Benjamin’s concept of the *flâneur* while walking these winter streets. How different it is to wander a city built for vehicles, where the simple act of walking becomes rebellion. Every step a small revolution against efficiency, against purpose, against the tyranny of destination. Along 17th Avenue, vacant storefronts wear “FOR LEASE” signs like prophecies of impermanence. The independent bookstores persist with stubborn grace: Shelf Life’s narrow aisles arrange themselves like desire paths through literature, Owl’s Nest Books occupies a strip mall location where paperbacks are arranged like prayers between cedar and sage. Books breathe here. Pages whisper. Spines crack like ice underfoot.

Persistence itself becomes an art form in a city like this. Every open independent bookstore is an act of defiance. Every local café that survives another winter is a miracle. Maybe that’s why we gather in these spaces like pilgrims. Maybe that’s why every table holds someone hunched over a laptop or notebook, creating.

#### The Economics of Hope

My latest self-published collection sold six copies last month — one to my mother, one to my ex, four to strangers who might be spam bots or particularly literary AI crawlers. Sometimes I dream of future scholars poring over my digital traces, analyzing line breaks and grocery lists for deeper meaning. More likely these words will end up in the vast digital landfill of forgotten texts, decomposing alongside abandoned blogs and unfinished novels. The silence of an empty Goodreads review page echoes louder than any critical acclaim. Numbers don’t lie. Stats tell stories. Amazon rankings become horoscopes. The walk between cafés changes with the seasons. Winter means hidden ice. Treacherous beauty. The risk of falling becomes part of the pilgrimage.

At Euphoria Café in Dalhousie, I used to order waffles that arrive like communion wafers drowned in maple syrup. The café’s walls display local art with hopeful price tags, each piece a testament to Calgary’s ecosystem of creativity. Yesterday, I watched a magpie build its nest from discarded mask elastic and Christmas lights — nature adapting to our detritus, finding beauty in our waste. Even the birds have learned to make art from what we leave behind, though their Yelp reviews remain suspiciously absent. The magpie watches me. I watch back. We’re both collecting shiny things to make meaning from.

This city runs on hope and caffeine. On dreams and deadlines. On the belief that if we just keep showing up, keep creating, keep trying, something will catch fire. Something will last.

#### Night Cities & Digital Dreams

Virginia Woolf haunts me in these spaces, her ghost asking what a room of one’s own means in a city where rent consumes nearly all our income. My brother’s apartment faces the alley where bottle pickers work their dawn patrol, their shopping carts orchestrating a symphony of glass and aluminum. They are more likely to be read about than to read, just as I am more likely to write about being read than to be read. At night, the Bow Building’s LED display runs test patterns that look like failed attempts at digital divination, corporate auguries spelling out futures I can’t quite decode through the musky haze of burning medicine. The city glows. It hums. It never really sleeps.

> Here’s what I know: words matter. Even unread ones. Even the ones that never leave our journals. Even the ones we write and delete and write again.

Every morning, I perform this ritual: writing words that may never find their readers. The draft folder on my laptop is graveyard of unfinished stories. Annie Dillard writes that how we spend our days is how we spend our lives — I spend mine watching clouds, crafting metaphors, pretending the difference between anonymity and fame is merely a matter of time rather than a law of thermodynamics. The universe tends toward entropy; most books tend toward remainder bins or that one box in Value Village marked “Inspirational.” Write anyway. Create anyway. Pay attention anyway. Time moves differently in bookstores. Minutes stretch like taffy. Hours collapse like old paperbacks. I lose whole afternoons in these spaces.

#### Between Places & Pages

The chinook arch forms above the city like a Rothko painting — bands of color dividing sky from meaning. Between Kensington’s Pages Books and Inglewood’s independent presses, I carry my notebook like a passport through territories of literature. Kierkegaard sits beside self-help bestsellers, the existential and the practical sharing shelf space in this liminal city, this place perpetually between mountain and prairie, between cultural relevance and obscurity, between genocidal Chapters and bankruptcy. The wind changes. The arch holds. The city waits.

> Let me be honest about something: most of us writing in these cafés will never be famous. Most of us will never see our books in these stores we haunt. Most of us will keep writing anyway.

My laptop holds ten years of journals, fifty thousand photos, three unpublished novels, and a folder labeled “Notes for Future Reference” that I haven’t opened since 2019. Each file a small illumination, a record of paying attention. The cursor blinks with my heartbeat as I type in cafés where baristas know my order but not my name, or that they might live in my writing. The sun sets by 4:30 PM in December, but the quality of darkness varies — tonight it’s the texture of wool, tomorrow might be velvet or raw silk, or that particular shade of black you get from accidentally closing all your browser tabs. Dark comes fast. Night stays long. Words keep coming.

#### Epilogue: On Faith & Following Through

We write to remember. We write to be remembered. Sometimes, we write just to prove we’re still here.

Somewhere, perhaps, six readers await my words. Somewhere, perhaps, a future wanderer will find in these digital traces a map of what it meant to live and write in this time, this beautiful obscurity. Until then, the coffee cools, the chinook winds erase yesterday’s snow, and I persist in this sacred practice of observation. The world exists first in our eyes, then in our words, then — if we’re lucky, if we’re patient, if we’re true — in the eyes of others. Even if those others number only six, and one is my mother rating it five stars “because I have to.”

This is what devotion looks like: showing up day after day, paying too much for coffee, filling notebooks no one may ever read. This is what faith looks like: believing that the act of creation matters, when the metrics suggest otherwise. This is what love looks like: choosing to stay, to write, to witness this city in all its mundane glory, even when no one else seems to be watching.
