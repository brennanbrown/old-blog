The first snow always falls at 3AM in Calgary. The city knows this in its bones, in the way certain truths become sacred through repetition – like convenience store fluorescence or the peculiar weight of grief that settles in your stomach on late-night transit rides. Tonight I’m watching it happen from inside a 7-Eleven on 17th Avenue, the only pulse of light for blocks. The clerk doesn’t look up from his phone. We’re both maintaining this delicate fiction that we’re not actually here, that this isn’t some kind of late-capitalist temple where the lost and lonely come to worship under buzzing lights. The hot dogs turn endlessly on their metal rollers like prayer wheels, marking time in their own greasy liturgy.

The snow is turning Calgary into a blank page – each flake erasing and rewriting the city beneath. I think about Roland Barthes alone in his apartment after his mother died, sorting through old photographs, realizing how memory works like snow: accumulating in drifts, smoothing rough edges, making the familiar strange. Each ending I’ve witnessed in this city has come with its own kind of snowfall. The barista who used to remember my order, gone one day without warning. The bookstore replaced by another cannabis shop. The friend who stopped answering texts. Beginnings are harder to recognize in the moment – they don’t announce themselves with the same ceremony.

The MAX Teal materializes out of the grey like some deep-sea creature, all metal and mist. I board, tap my transit card. The sound echoes through the empty bus like a door closing in an empty house. The driver and I perform our ritual of mutual invisibility – this theater of public transit after midnight where everyone pretends they’re somewhere else, suspended in what anthropologists might call liminal space but what really feels more like purgatory with better lighting. Through fogged windows, Crowchild Trail unspools beneath us, road signs glowing like false moons in the snow-heavy sky.

Somewhere past Mount Royal University, where the city starts to forget itself, I think about Maurice Blanchot’s “other night” – not the darkness that follows day, but something more fundamental, the darkness that precedes being itself. That’s what grief feels like: not the absence of what was lost, but the presence of an absence. It pools in places like this – empty buses, convenience stores, the spaces between streetlights where anything feels possible precisely because nothing is happening. We carry our dead in our pockets like spare change, weighing us down until we learn to walk differently.

Richmond Road stretches out like a scar through Killarney. The neighborhood holds its breath – no cars, no porch lights, no movement except the steady accumulation of snow and the ghost of my footprints immediately filling in behind me. In the summer, this street belongs to the magpies, those tuxedoed prophets of territorial dispute. But tonight it belongs to no one, or maybe to everyone who’s ever walked it alone, adding their solitude to its memory like layers of sediment.

Walking these streets feels like being the last person on Earth, or maybe the first – Adam before names, before the weight of meaning. Each house sits dark and patient as a confessional booth. The snow makes everything quieter than quiet, muffling the usual suburban soundtrack – the hum of furnaces, the distant cry of trains along the CP rail line, the whisper of cars on Crowchild. In this silence, grief becomes a physical presence, walking beside me like a faithful dog. We’ve grown comfortable with each other, the way you do with any chronic condition. It follows me home from the 7-Eleven pilgrimages, sits with me on late-night buses, keeps me company on these walks through neighborhoods I’ve known so long they feel like extended family – distant cousins I see only at funerals and holidays.

Near my house, I pass the Catholic church where someone has left the Christmas lights up all year. They’re dark now, but I know they’re there – tangled and patient, waiting for their season to return. Simone Weil wrote that attention is the rarest form of generosity, but she never specified what we should pay attention to. Maybe it’s enough to notice the way snow falls differently around streetlights, how it turns the ordinary geometry of the city into something sacred and strange. Each flake carries a memory: convenience store coffee, empty bus seats, streets named after people no one remembers. The grief in my pocket grows lighter with each step, transforming slowly into something else – not hope exactly, but its quiet sister, endurance.

I reach my front steps, where tomorrow’s footprints haven’t happened yet. The key turns in the lock – a sound so familiar it’s become invisible, like your own heartbeat or the weight of everything you’ve lost. Inside, the darkness holds the shape of home. I stand at the window watching the snow erase the city, thinking about how every ending is just a different kind of beginning, how grief is just love learning to walk backward, how somewhere in this city another person is standing in a 7-Eleven, buying coffee they won’t drink, waiting for the next bus to carry them through this beautiful, impossible night. We are all performing the same ritual: stepping into the darkness, trusting the light to find us again.
